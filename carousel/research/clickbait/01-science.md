# 01 - The science of the click: what makes a headline / cover line stop the scroll

Research file for the TikTok photo-carousel cover line. Compiled 10.10.2026 from primary sources
(papers, datasets, the authors' own PDFs). Where only a secondary report was reachable, that is said
explicitly next to the claim. Quotes are verbatim; numbers are as printed by the authors.

## How to read this file

**Evidence grades (used on every claim):**

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **A** | Randomised, and either replicated or pre-registered with a held-out confirmatory test |
| **B** | Large observational dataset (thousands+), OR a large randomised dataset that is single-source / not replicated / contradicted elsewhere |
| **C** | Small study, single experiment, survey of intentions, or one platform with heavy confounds |
| **D** | Opinion, vendor blog, undocumented industry number, theory without a click test |

**Three facts to hold in mind before any rule:**

1. **Word-level headline features move clicks by single-digit percent (relative).** On Upworthy, one
   extra negative word = +2.3% relative CTR; most textual constructs move CTR by at most +/-3.1% per SD.
   A model built on linguistic features picks the better of two headlines only 54.4% of the time.
   What the headline is *about* matters far more than how it is dressed. (Sections 2, 4.)
2. **Almost all the big randomised evidence is from news headlines on websites** (Upworthy, Chartbeat,
   Blendle). Nobody has published a randomised test of text on a TikTok carousel cover. Every rule at
   the end is a transfer, and is graded as such.
3. **The famous "clickbait features" were mostly derived by describing clickbait, not by testing it.**
   Corpora (Chakraborty, Potthast, Blom & Hansen) tell you what clickbait *looks like*; the
   experiments that tested those features (Molina et al. 2021, Kuiken et al. 2017, Scacco & Muddiman)
   found many of them do *not* raise clicks, and some lower them.

## 1. Curiosity: the information-gap theory and its tests

### 1.1 Loewenstein 1994 - the theory (Grade D as theory; it is the frame everyone tests)

George Loewenstein, "The psychology of curiosity: A review and reinterpretation", *Psychological
Bulletin* 116(1), 75-98. PDF: https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/PsychofCuriosity.pdf
(scanned; quotes transcribed from p. 87-92).

- The mechanism: "the information-gap theory views curiosity as arising when attention becomes focused
  on a gap in one's knowledge. Such information gaps produce the feeling of deprivation labeled
  *curiosity*. The curious individual is motivated to obtain the missing information to reduce or
  eliminate the feeling of deprivation." (p. 87)
- Specificity: curiosity "should be positively related to its ability to resolve uncertainty (i.e., to
  close the information gap)" and "curiosity should be greater for insight than for incremental
  problems" (p. 88) - i.e. a gap that one piece of information closes beats a gap that needs a lot.
- Need a base of knowledge first: "curiosity is unlikely to arise in the absence of an existing
  knowledge base and that the likelihood of experiencing curiosity should increase as an individual
  obtains information about a particular topic." (p. 89)
- Too-big / unreachable gaps do not attract: "people will not expose themselves to curiosity-inducing
  situations in which there is only a slim chance of satisfying the curiosity or in which there would
  be a long delay before the information is received." (p. 90)
- The five situational triggers (p. 91), verbatim headings: "1. The posing of a question or
  presentation of a riddle or puzzle ... 2. Exposure to a sequence of events with an anticipated but
  unknown resolution ... 3. The violation of expectations ... 4. Possession of information by someone
  else ... 5. Past attainments can serve as a reference point" (the tip-of-the-tongue / "you knew this"
  case).
- The built-in disappointment: "the disappointment often experienced when curiosity is satisfied can
  also be explained by the fact that curiosity is driven by deprivation. Eliminating curiosity
  eliminates the deprivation but leaves one in a neutral hedonic state." (p. 92)

### 1.2 Kang et al. 2009 - "The wick in the candle of learning" (Grade C; small N, but the inverted-U
has since been seen in other labs)

Kang, Hsu, Krajbich, Loewenstein, McClure, Wang, Camerer, *Psychological Science* 20(8).
PDF: https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf

- Samples: fMRI study 19 Caltech students, 40 trivia questions; replication 16 students; behavioural
  follow-up 10 (token) + 20 (time) students.
- Verbatim: "Reported curiosity was indeed an inverted-U-shaped function of P, reaching its maximum when
  P was around .50 (Fig. 1c). Curiosity correlated positively with P(1 - P), r = .44, p < .0005 ... estimated
  peak curiosity was at values of P between .45 and .55 in three quarters of the subjects."
  (P = the reader's own confidence that their guess is right.)
- Behaviour, not just self-report: "subjects spent more scarce resources (either limited tokens or
  waiting time) to find out answers when they were more curious."
- Authors' summary: "curiosity increases with uncertainty (up to a point) suggests that a small amount
  of knowledge can pique curiosity".

**Translation for a cover line:** maximum pull when the viewer feels ~50/50 about the answer - they can
half-guess it but are not sure.

### 1.3 Wade & Kidd 2019 - "the role of prior knowledge and curiosity in learning" (Grade C+)

*Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 26:1377-1387. PDF:
https://www.kiddlab.com/_files/ugd/0975fd_fe8691bfabc3409b96b14d668cc6bf39.pdf

- 114 MTurk participants recruited, 87 analysed, 100 trivia questions (5,973 trials).
- "Curiosity in our task is best predicted by a learner's estimate of their current knowledge, more so
  than an objective measure of what they actually know."
- Effect size: curiosity vs *believed* closeness r(5971) = 0.23; vs *objective* closeness r = 0.09
  ("the variance explained by the relationship was negligible (<1% of variance)").
- "learners' curiosity should be piqued when they come to believe that they are close to knowing the
  answer".

**Translation:** it is the *feeling* of "I almost know this" that pulls, not actual knowledge. A cover
should make the viewer feel they nearly have it.

### 1.4 Goldilocks / too-big gaps backfire (Grade B for the principle across labs; each study C)

- Kidd, Piantadosi & Aslin 2012, *PLoS ONE* (infants, 2 experiments):
  "Infants' probability of looking away was greatest on stimulus items whose complexity ... was either
  very low or very high." https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0036399
- Poli, Meyer, Mars & Hunnius 2022, *Cognition*: exploration "is guided by learning progress and
  perceptual novelty ... we demonstrate an overall tendency of participants to avoid extreme forms of
  uncertainty." https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2022.105119
- Baranes, Oudeyer & Gottlieb 2015, *Vision Research* (Gottlieb lab, eye-tracking): "higher curiosity was
  associated with earlier anticipatory orienting of gaze toward the answer location ... distinct from
  those produced by variations in confidence and surprise." https://doi.org/10.1016/j.visres.2015.10.009
  (Shows curiosity is visible in where the eyes go - i.e. it drives looking, the exact behaviour a cover
  needs.)
- Menon & Soman 2002, *Journal of Advertising* 31(3) (simulated web-ad experiment with clickstream):
  recommend "(1) curiosity generation by highlighting a gap in extant knowledge, (2) the presence of a
  hint to guide elaboration for curiosity resolution, (3) sufficient time to try and resolve curiosity
  as well as the assurance of curiosity-resolving information". Also: "curiosity does not dramatically
  increase the observed quantity of search ... it seems to improve the quality of search substantially".
  https://doi.org/10.1080/00913367.2002.10673672
  - A widely repeated claim that their **medium-gap ad drew "twice as much interest"** than narrow or
    wide gaps comes from a blog summary (bradenkelley.com), not from the abstract I could read.
    Treat the "2x" as **D** until the paper's table is checked.

### 1.5 What makes a gap pull harder (Grade A- to B: multiple pre-registered / multi-experiment papers)

- **Salience and recency of the gap.** Golman, Loewenstein, Molnar & Saccardo, "The Demand for, and
  Avoidance of, Information", *Management Science* 2022. Five experiments, n = 2,361: "People are
  indeed more inclined to acquire information (a) when it feels more important, even if it cannot aid
  decision making ...; (b) when a question is more salient, manipulated through time lag ...; and (c) when
  anticipated beliefs have higher valence". https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2021.4244
  - Co-author Molnar on clickbait (CMU press release, 2021): "clickbait creates a feeling of deprivation
    out of thin air"; Golman: "It is not enough to get people to think about the topic generally. You
    want them to think specifically about what they don't know."
    https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/news/news-stories/2021/december/golman-click.html
  - Note (c): people *seek* information they expect to be **good** news and avoid what they expect to
    be bad - a direct tension with the "negativity sells" literature in section 4.1.
- **Asking for a prediction raises curiosity.** Brod & Breitwieser 2019, *npj Science of Learning*:
  "More facts received high-curiosity ratings in the prediction condition, which indicates that
  generating predictions stimulated curiosity ... generating a prediction stimulates curiosity by
  increasing the relevance of the knowledge gap." (Grade C - one lab study, pupillometry-backed.)
  https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-019-0056-y
- **Teasing then resolving is net-positive, and people under-use it.** Ruan, Hsee & Lu 2018, *Journal of
  Marketing Research*, seven studies: "trivia readers report better hedonic experiences if they are
  first teased with some missing information and then given that information than if they receive all
  the information at the same time ... imbuing an ad with an uncertainty creation-resolution process
  improves the viewer's attitude toward and increases the viewer's willingness to try the advertised
  product." (Grade A- : 7 studies, one team.) https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.15.0346
- **Curiosity is strong enough to override self-interest.** Hsee & Ruan 2016, "The Pandora Effect",
  *Psychological Science*, 4 experiments: "curiosity could lead humans to expose themselves to aversive
  stimuli (even electric shocks) for no apparent benefits." (Grade B-.)
  https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797616631733

### 1.6 Curiosity in real news clicks (Grade B)

Qiu & Golman 2024, "Curiosity in news consumption", *Applied Cognitive Psychology*
https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.4195 - over 100,000 WeChat news articles:

- "people tend to consume news when: the headline sparks a salient question; the content appears more
  important (e.g., emphasized by the headline's position on the webpage or an exclamation mark); the
  headline refers to more surprising topics ...; and the headline has lower valence."
- The cost: "our data also reveal a small negative correlation between the number of clicks and the
  ratio of likes to clicks, suggesting that while inducing curiosity can drive short-term news
  consumption, it doesn't necessarily enhance long-term reader engagement."

**Section verdict (Grade B overall):** the information gap is real and causal in the lab; it peaks at
*intermediate* uncertainty and when the viewer *feels* close; it fails when the gap is vague, too big,
or the payoff seems unreachable. Clicks gained this way can cost likes/trust (section 6).

## 2. The Upworthy Research Archive and the analyses built on it

### 2.1 The dataset (Grade A as a source: real randomised field tests)

Matias, Munger, Aubin Le Quere & Ebersole 2021, "The Upworthy Research Archive, a time series of 32,487
experiments in U.S. media", *Scientific Data*. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41597-021-00934-7 ;
archive: https://upworthy.natematias.com/

- "Each experiment tested variations in a headline and image 'package'"; "The dataset includes a total
  of 538,272,878 impressions and 8,182,674 clicks." Average 5 packages per test; Jan 2013 - Apr 2015.
- Split for honest science: exploratory 4,873 tests, confirmatory 22,743 tests; publications must be
  registered reports. The archive warns: "Multiple comparisons and overfitting represent serious risks
  to scientific understanding with a dataset of this size."
- Caveat: average CTR is ~1.6% (Banerjee & Urminsky); Upworthy's audience and style were unusual
  (positive "viral" stories), and images are not in the archive.

### 2.2 Robertson et al. 2023 - "Negativity drives online news consumption" (Grade A within Upworthy;
B as a general law because the Chartbeat replication in 2.4 found no average effect)

*Nature Human Behaviour* 7, 812-822. Open PDF: https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/94951/1/s41562-023-01538-4.pdf
(Registered report, confirmatory set; 12,448 RCTs after filtering, 53,699 headlines; ~5.7M clicks,
>370M impressions.)

- "For a headline of average length, each additional negative word increased the click-through rate by
  2.3%."
- "for each positive word in a headline of average length, the CTR decreases by 1.0%."
- Discrete emotions: "A one standard deviation increase in sadness increases the odds of a user clicking
  the headline by 0.7%, while a one standard deviation increase in joy or fear decreases the odds of a
  user clicking on a headline by 0.9% and 0.7%, respectively." Anger: not significant (P = 0.666).
- Length: "A longer news headline increases the CTR ... lengthier and less complex formulations are
  appealing to users" (Upworthy average headline = 14.965 words).
- Authors' own limitation: "Upworthy differs from more traditional news sources due to its use of
  'click-bait' headlines."
- Most common negative word in the sample: "wrong" (n = 728).

### 2.3 Gligoric, Lifchits, West & Anderson 2023 - linguistic features (Grade A: pre-registered,
held-out confirmatory 24,333 headline pairs)

"Linguistic effects on news headline success: Evidence from thousands of online field experiments
(Registered Report)", *PLOS ONE* 18(3): e0281682. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0281682

- Ceiling on predictability: "prediction accuracy on the Confirmatory Dataset is 54.42%". A coin flip
  is 50%. Linguistic features matter, but weakly.
- Supported in confirmatory data (logit coefficients, pair-level):
  - negative-emotion words: beta = 0.18, p = 1.6e-14 (positive)
  - length (characters): beta = 0.07 (longer wins)
  - indefinite article ("a/an", generality): beta = 0.12
  - first-person singular ("I/my"): beta = 0.24
  - third-person singular ("she/his"): beta = 0.22; third-person plural: beta = 0.09
  - first-person plural ("we/our"): beta = -0.15 (hurts)
- **Not supported:** positive-emotion words hurt (beta = -0.04, n.s.); readability; the definite article;
  and "**we found no significant positive association between the use of second-person pronouns and
  headline success (H7; beta = 0.05, p = 4.37x10-2)**" (fails the pre-registered p < .01 bar).

### 2.4 Banerjee & Urminsky - two papers

**(a) "The Language That Drives Engagement: A Systematic Large-scale Analysis of Headline
Experiments", *Marketing Science* 44(3), 2025.** https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2021.0018
Published abstract: "the use of textual cues identified in previous research and industry advice does
impact the effectiveness of headlines overall, but the prior research and industry advice does not
always provide useful guidance as to the direction of the effects."

The final paper is paywalled. The authors' 2021 working paper (exploratory data only) is open and
is stamped "THIS VERSION REPORTS PRELIMINARY RESULTS FROM AN EXPLORATORY DATASET ... FINAL RESULTS FROM
CONFIRMATORY DATASET ARE LIKELY TO DIFFER" (https://home.uchicago.edu/ourminsky/Banerjee_Urminsky_Headlines.pdf).
Its findings, therefore **Grade C until matched to the published version**:

- Size: "Given the average click-through rate of 1.6%, most of the estimated effects ranged from a 3.1%
  decrease to a 3.1% increase in click-through rates for a one standard deviation change".
- Direction of folk wisdom: "In the Bonferroni-corrected model, a similar number of effects (5) were
  significant in the predicted direction as in the opposite direction (4)."
- Forward reference works: "using forward reference language in headlines ... increased engagement ...
  incorporating cues such as unresolved pronouns motivated readers to click through".
- Promises to answer/teach backfired: "headlines that incorporated information-seeking cues (e.g., by
  suggesting that the content would answer a question or teach something) performed more poorly."
- "the presence of numbers in headlines increased click-through rates"; "making headlines more concrete
  improved engagement"; "headlines implying social stories about individuals were more effective, but
  writing headlines directed to the reader backfired."
- "Using more emotionally intense language increased engagement. However, including more positive
  emotion words in headlines reduced engagement".

**(b) "Rethinking the Negativity Bias Online" (2025 preprint).** https://doi.org/10.31219/osf.io/fxp7g_v1
Chartbeat data: "over 150,000 randomized A/B tests conducted on 398 English-language news sites
worldwide", plus five pre-registered experiments (N = 8,369). **Grade A- for the null average + the
heterogeneity (randomised, huge, multi-site); preprint, not yet peer reviewed.**

- "negatively worded headlines showed no average effect on engagement, but revealed stark heterogeneity
  across platforms."
- What *did* replicate across 398 sites: "Positive Tone ... had a negative effect on click-through rates,
  and Intensity of Emotion ... had a positive effect".
- The moderator: "under credibility-seeking goals, negative language triggers inferences of
  manipulative intent, suppressing engagement ... By contrast, under enjoyment goals, the same negative
  language may increase appeal to readers."

**Section verdict:** two things replicate across Upworthy *and* 398 Chartbeat sites - **positive/upbeat
wording slightly hurts, emotional intensity slightly helps** (Grade A-). "Negativity sells" is true on
Upworthy and *not* on average elsewhere (Grade B, context-dependent).

## 3. Clickbait corpora and linguistics (what clickbait LOOKS like - not proof that it works)

### 3.1 Chakraborty, Paranjape, Kakarla & Ganguly 2016 - "Stop Clickbait" (Grade B as description)

ASONAM 2016. https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.09786 - 7,623 human-labelled clickbait headlines (BuzzFeed,
Upworthy, ViralNova, Scoopwhoop, ViralStories; Fleiss kappa 0.79) vs 18,513 Wikinews headlines.

- Length: "the average length of the clickbait headlines is 10, whereas the average length is 7 for
  non-clickbait headlines"; but shorter words (4.5 vs 6 characters).
- Contractions: "only 0.6% of the traditional news headlines contain word shortenings, whereas nearly
  22% of clickbait headlines have such shortened words."
- Stop words "45% compared to 18%"; hyperbolic words ("Awe-inspiring, breathtakingly, gut-wrenching");
  determiners "their, my, which, these ... to make the user inquisitive about the object being
  referenced"; direct address "I, We, You".
- Clickbait is formulaic: "the proportion of headlines that contained the top 0.05% of unique n-grams in
  clickbaits was 65%, whereas in non-clickbaits, the proportion was 19%."
- Classifier: 93% accuracy. **This tells you the style is recognisable - including by platforms and by
  users - not that it earns clicks.**

### 3.2 Potthast et al. - Webis Clickbait corpora (Grade B as description)

- Potthast, Kopsel, Stein & Hagen 2016, "Clickbait Detection", ECIR (best short paper). 2,992 tweets
  from the 20 most-retweeted publishers, week 24 of 2015: 767 (26%) judged clickbait. Their stated
  rationale is Loewenstein: "teaser messages opening a so-called 'curiosity gap'". Best model ROC-AUC
  0.74. https://downloads.webis.de/publications/papers/potthast_2016b.pdf
- Potthast et al. 2018, "Crowdsourcing a Large Corpus of Clickbait on Twitter" (Webis-Clickbait-17):
  "38,517 annotated Twitter tweets ... from the top 27 most retweeted news publishers ... annotated on
  4-point scale by five annotators". Used for the Clickbait Challenge 2017 (13 detectors).
  https://webis.de/data/webis-clickbait-17.html ; https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.10847
- No click data in either corpus.

### 3.3 Blom & Hansen 2015 - forward reference (Grade B as description; no click data)

"Click bait: Forward-reference as lure in online news headlines", *Journal of Pragmatics* 76:87-100.
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pragma.2014.11.010

- Abstract: "This is why you should read this article. Although such an opening statement does not make
  much sense read in isolation, journalists often write headlines like this on news websites. They use
  the forward-referring technique as a stylistic and narrative luring device trying to induce
  anticipation and curiosity ... we map the use of forward-referring headlines in online news journalism
  by conducting an analysis of 100,000 headlines from 10 different Danish news websites. The results
  show that commercialization and tabloidization seem to lead to a recurrent use of forward-reference".
- The devices they catalogue: demonstrative pronouns ("this"), personal pronouns without antecedent
  ("he", "she"), adverbs ("here"), definite articles for unintroduced things, ellipsis, imperatives,
  interrogatives, and general nouns ("the reason", "the trick").

### 3.4 Scott 2021 - why it works, in relevance theory (Grade D: theory + corpus, no click test)

"You won't believe what's in this paper! Clickbait, relevance and the curiosity gap", *Journal of
Pragmatics* 175:53-66. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pragma.2020.12.023

- Two over-represented categories: "(i) definite referring expressions and (ii) superlatives and
  intensifiers ... these parts-of-speech contribute to an information gap by encouraging readers to
  construct new conceptual files based on the terms used in the headline, while providing little or no
  content for those files."

### 3.5 Other corpora worth knowing

- Ha et al. 2018 (ICWSM), Instagram fashion posts: "approximately 11% of fashion-related Instagram posts
  are clickbait". https://doi.org/10.1609/icwsm.v12i1.15019 (B, descriptive)
- Zannettou et al. 2018, 206k YouTube videos: "YouTube recommendation engine does not take into account
  clickbait." https://doi.org/10.1109/spw.2018.00018 (B, descriptive; 2018 state)

## 4. Feature by feature: what was actually tested

### 4.1 Negative vs positive, emotion words, superlatives

| Finding | Source | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| +2.3% CTR per negative word; -1.0% per positive word (Upworthy) | Robertson 2023 | A (Upworthy) |
| Negative-emotion words win pairwise (beta 0.18); positive n.s. | Gligoric 2023 | A |
| Negativity: **no average effect** across 398 sites; positive tone hurts; emotional intensity helps | Banerjee & Urminsky 2025 preprint | A- |
| Negative words backfire for credibility-seeking readers (manipulative intent) | same, 5 pre-registered exps | A- |
| Sadness +0.7%/SD; fear -0.7%; joy -0.9%; anger n.s. | Robertson 2023 | A (Upworthy) |
| Negative sentimental word: +17 percentage points "performance" (Blendle newsletter) | Kuiken 2017 | C |
| Negative superlatives +30%, positive superlatives -29% vs neither | Outbrain blog (via Poynter 2014), no method published | D |
| Positive AND negative superlatives drew fewer "read more" clicks than plain headlines (odds 1.68x and 1.81x in favour of plain) | Molina 2021 Study 1, N = 149 | C |
| Emotional arousal (awe, anger, anxiety) drives *sharing*; sadness reduces sharing | Berger & Milkman 2012, ~3 months of NYT articles + experiments | B+ |
| Moral-emotional words +20% retweets per word | Brady 2017, n = 563,312 tweets | B, contested |
| ...but "moral contagion model performs no better than an implausible XYZ contagion model" | Burton, Cruz & Hahn 2021 | B (critique) |
| Out-group words +67% odds of share per term | Rathje 2021, 2.7M posts | B (politics only) |

Verbatim anchors:
- Outbrain (Poynter, https://www.poynter.org/reporting-editing/2014/the-worst-news-ever-negative-headlines-outperform-positive-ones/):
  "Headlines with negative superlatives performed 30% better ... The average click-through rate on
  headlines with negative superlatives was a staggering 63% higher than that of their positive
  counterparts." No sample, period or method is published in the article.
- Berger & Milkman, *JMR* 49(2) https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.10.0353 : "Content that evokes high-arousal
  positive (awe) or negative (anger or anxiety) emotions is more viral. Content that evokes low-arousal,
  or deactivating, emotions (e.g., sadness) is less viral." (Note: Robertson found sadness *helps clicks*
  - clicking and sharing are different behaviours; Robertson says so: "Sharing is a very public
  behavior".)
- Brady et al. 2017 *PNAS* https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1618923114 ; Burton et al. 2021 *Nature Human
  Behaviour* https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-021-01133-5 ; Rathje, Van Bavel & van der Linden 2021 *PNAS*
  https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2024292118

**Verdict:** robust = "upbeat/positive adjectives do not help and slightly hurt" and "emotional
*intensity* helps" (A-). Negativity = helps on entertainment-oriented audiences, neutral or harmful
where people want reliable information (A- for the heterogeneity). Superlatives = no good evidence they
help; the only numbers in their favour are vendor numbers (D).

### 4.2 Question headlines and "Betteridge's law"

"Betteridge's law" ("any headline that ends in a question mark can be answered by the word no") is a
journalist's adage - **D**. The tests:

| Finding | Source | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Questions beat statements; self-referencing questions best (+175% clicks Twitter; +257% Finn.no) | Lai & Farbrot 2014, 2 small field experiments | C |
| Questions: -17 percentage points in performance (Blendle) | Kuiken 2017 | C |
| Plain headline had 1.97x the odds of a click vs a question headline | Molina 2021 Study 1 | C |
| Question headlines: more negative reaction, lower expected engagement (N = 2,057) | Scacco & Muddiman 2016 (ENP) | C |
| "Answer a question / teach something" cues performed worse | Banerjee & Urminsky prelim | C |
| Question titles: downloaded more but cited less (2,172 PLoS papers) | Jamali & Nikzad 2011 | B- |
| Question marks: "little or no impact" (Frontiers in Psychology titles, Altmetric) | Lockwood 2016 | C |

Verbatim:
- Lai & Farbrot, *Social Influence* 9(4), https://doi.org/10.1080/15534510.2013.847859 : "question
  headlines are significantly more effective than declarative headlines in generating readership ...
  However, results also suggest that the effect of question headlines varies across message topics."
  The Twitter feed had 6,350 followers; on Finn.no "question headlines for washing machines actually led
  to fewer clicks than statement headlines" (BPS Research Digest summary,
  https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/are-you-more-likely-click-headlines-are-phrased-question).
- Kuiken et al. (Table 5): "Headlines that contain a question are preferred over headlines that do not ...
  p = 0.019 ... -17"; text: "headlines that do not contain a question achieve better results, contradictory
  to the hypothesis."
- Scacco & Muddiman (journalism.co.uk report): question headlines "prompt a more negative response from
  readers as well as a more negative expectation of the story that follows" and "less expected
  engagement with the story than traditional or forward-reference headlines."
  https://www.journalism.co.uk/readers-perceive-question-based-headlines-more-negatively-study-shows/
- Jamali & Nikzad, *Scientometrics* https://doi.org/10.1007/s11192-011-0412-z : "articles with question
  titles tended to be downloaded more but cited less than the others."

**Verdict (C, leaning negative):** the only positive result is small and topic-dependent; four
independent tests found questions neutral or worse. A *statement that implies* the question beats the
question itself.

### 4.3 Numbers, lists, odd vs even

| Finding | Source | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Numbers increased CTR (Upworthy, exploratory) | Banerjee & Urminsky prelim | C |
| "Headlines that contain a number" - p = 0.202, no effect | Kuiken 2017 | C |
| Lists were as good as plain headlines; lists beat questions (OR 1.63) and modals (OR 1.83) | Molina 2021 Study 1 | C |
| "numbers 3-10 driving peak engagement ... no strong preference towards odd or even numbers" (100M headlines, shares) | BuzzSumo 2020, https://buzzsumo.com/blog/most-shared-headlines-study/ | C/D (vendor, observational) |
| Odd numbers +20% CTR vs even (150,000 headlines) | Attributed to Outbrain via Content Marketing Institute; no primary report found | D |

**Verdict:** a concrete number is at worst harmless and possibly helpful (C). **Odd beats even is folklore
(D)** - the one large dataset that looked (BuzzSumo) found no preference, and the source of the "+20%"
could not be found.

### 4.4 "You" / second person

| Finding | Source | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| No significant effect of "you" (pre-registered H7 failed) | Gligoric 2023 | A (null) |
| "writing headlines directed to the reader backfired" | Banerjee & Urminsky prelim | C |
| Self-referencing questions best | Lai & Farbrot 2014 | C |
| Personal/possessive pronouns +25 pp; starting with a pronoun +20 pp | Kuiken 2017 | C |
| More "you" in songs = liked and bought more (4,200 Billboard songs + N = 2,921 experiments) - works by making people think of someone in their life | Packard & Berger 2020 *Psych Science* https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797620902380 | B (different medium) |
| Third person singular ("she", "his") helps (beta 0.22); "social stories about individuals" help | Gligoric 2023; Banerjee prelim | A / C |

**Verdict:** "you" is **not** a proven click lever in headlines (A-grade null). A story about a specific
other person is better supported.

### 4.5 Length

- Upworthy: longer headlines win (Robertson beta = 0.040; Gligoric beta = 0.07; Banerjee prelim "higher
  word count were clicked on more") - **A, but for 15-word web headlines read at leisure.**
- Kuiken: ">50 characters" n.s.; *shorter words* (<=7 characters per word) +33 pp.
- Jung et al. 2022 (Facebook): "a negative effect of longer words for headlines".
- Jamali & Nikzad: "Articles with longer titles were downloaded slightly less".
- Clickbait itself is longer than news (10 vs 7 words, Chakraborty).

**Verdict for a feed cover:** the "longer is better" result is about a different reading situation (a
headline list on a website). For a cover seen for ~1.7 s (section 7) the binding constraint is reading
time, so the transfer is: **short words, few of them.** (Derived - C.)

### 4.6 Specificity, concreteness, information content

- Banerjee prelim: "making headlines more concrete improved engagement" (C).
- Gligoric: *indefinite* article (generality, "a mom", "a teacher") beta = 0.12 - a concrete but
  unnamed character (A).
- Facebook survey 2014: "80% of the time people preferred headlines that helped them decide if they
  wanted to read the full article before they had to click through."
  https://about.fb.com/news/2014/08/news-feed-fyi-click-baiting/ (C: platform survey, method unpublished)
- Scacco & Muddiman 2019/20, *New Media & Society*: "summary headlines heighten perceptions of headline
  information adequacy ... Study 2 determines that individuals' selection behavior on nine local
  newspaper websites also favors summary headlines." https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444819863408
  (B-: field data; a secondary report gives 6,701 vs 5,324 page views for summary vs curiosity over
  5,288 political headlines - mappingignorance.org - not verified in the paper itself.)
- Tafesse 2020, *Internet Research*, 4,398 trending YouTube videos: "greater availability of information
  in video titles is negatively associated with video views". https://doi.org/10.1108/INTR-10-2019-0406 (B-)
- Golman: "think specifically about what they don't know."

**Verdict:** concrete subject + withheld specific answer. Vague gaps ("this will change everything")
and fully-spelled-out summaries both lose to "a specific thing you can picture, with one specific
detail missing". (B, by triangulation.)

### 4.7 Forward reference / deixis ("this", "here's why", "the reason")

- Banerjee prelim: forward reference increased CTR (C, pending final paper).
- Molina 2021: demonstratives ("this, these, here") did as well as plain headlines - not better (C).
- Scacco & Muddiman 2016: forward-reference headlines showed "no difference in perception" vs
  traditional ones (C).
- Kuiken: signal words ("dit/deze/waarom/hoe" = this/these/why/how) +16 pp (C).
- Jung et al. 2022, 4,400+ Facebook posts: common clickbait phrases in headlines "were statistically
  significantly associated with a loss of roughly a quarter reactions, shares and comments"
  https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0266743 (B-).

**Verdict:** mild forward reference (an unresolved "this"/"she"/"the reason") is neutral-to-positive
(C). The *stock* clickbait phrases ("you won't believe", "what happened next") are recognised and cost
engagement (B-), and are exactly what platforms detect (section 6).

### 4.8 Punctuation, capitals, emoji

- Cui, Chung, Peng & Wang 2024, *Journal of Business Research* (16,215 YouTube video covers): "strong
  sentiments in thumbnails, whether positive or negative, lead to more views, whereas strong sentiments
  in captions have the opposite effect. Video titles with positive rather than negative sentiments
  generate more views. Sentiment markers such as punctuation, all capitals, and emoticons all contribute
  to more clicks." https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2024.114849 (abstract via
  https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbrese/v183y2024ics0148296324003539.html) (B: observational)
- Jung et al. 2022: "unusual punctuation in the headline ... caused 55.0% more reactions"; the same
  feature in the post text "decreased shares by 44.0%". (B-)
- Qiu & Golman 2024: exclamation mark read as importance, more clicks. (B)

**Verdict:** visual emphasis markers *on the image* are associated with more views (B, observational);
the same intensity in the caption/body text is associated with less. Fits the "loud cover, calm
caption" pattern.

## 5. Experiments that tested "clickbait" as a whole

| Study | Design | Result | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kuiken et al. 2017, *Digital Journalism* | 1,828 Blendle original vs rewritten headline pairs + newsletter CTR, position-corrected | most features help 14-33 pp, questions/quotes hurt; "None of the regression models performed significantly better than the baseline" | C/B- |
| Molina et al. 2021, CHI | Study 1 N = 149 (7 clickbait types vs plain); Study 2 N = 249, one story, 8 headline versions | "clickbait did not generate more curiosity than non-clickbait"; Study 2 null on clicks (Wald chi2 = 4.42, p = .73); detectors agree only 47% | C |
| Scacco & Muddiman 2019/20 | lab + field (9 local news sites) | summary headlines beat curiosity headlines | B- |

| Munger, Luca, Nagler & Tucker 2020, *POQ* | Facebook-ad + MTurk samples, randomised clickbait vs traditional | "older people and non-Democrats have a higher 'preference for clickbait,' but reading clickbait headlines does not drive affective polarization, information retention, or trust in media." | B- |

Key verbatim lines:
- Kuiken (https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/20924776/Effective_Headlines_of_Newspaper_Articles_in_a_Digital_Environment.pdf):
  "With hindsight, we can say that we were too optimistic about the predictive value of the headline
  features just by themselves. The topic and genre of the news article the headline was referring to may
  have such a large impact on the CTR that our subtle differences in headline features disappear."
- Molina (https://pike.psu.edu/publications/chi21.pdf): "the odds of non-clickbait headlines receiving a
  click was 2.22 times greater than modal headlines (p <.001), 1.81 times greater than negative
  superlatives (p= .01), 1.68 times greater than positive superlatives (p= .03), and 1.97 times greater
  than questions (p= .003). On the other hand, non-clickbait headlines were as successful as
  demonstrative adjectives, lists, and 'wh' headlines."
- Apresjan & Orlov (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pragma.2022.02.003): "not all clickbait headlines are
  effective: in order to attract clicks, clickbait must not be obviously false and must increase the
  potential appeal and importance of insufficiently engaging news pieces on topics that do not require
  specialized knowledge."
- Munger et al.: https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfaa008

**Section verdict (B):** "clickbait" as a bundle is *not* reliably more clickable. What survives is the
gap mechanism itself, when the gap is about something already interesting and the headline is not
obviously overselling.

## 6. Backlash: when the bait costs you

| Finding | Source | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Clickbait articles get "far fewer shares" (19,386 articles, 27 publishers + experiment); clickbait read as manipulative -> source derogation | Mukherjee, Dutta & De Bruyn 2022, *JAMS* | B+ |
| Negativity triggers "inferences of manipulative intent" for credibility-seeking readers | Banerjee & Urminsky 2025 | A- |
| Curiosity headlines: more clicks but lower likes-per-click | Qiu & Golman 2024 | B |
| Clickbait headlines lower quality/credibility ratings, small effects (N = 483, 413) | Molyneux & Coddington 2020 | C |
| No effect on trust in media, polarisation, retention | Munger et al. 2020 | B- |
| Platform demotion of headlines that withhold or exaggerate | Facebook 2014, 2016 | policy fact |

Verbatim:
- Mukherjee et al. (https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-021-00830-x): "we predict that clickbait tactics may be
  perceived by some readers as a manipulative attempt, leading to source derogation where the publisher
  may be perceived as less competent and trustworthy ... Using a controlled experiment, we confirm that
  high-emotional headlines are shared more and show evidence that clickbait often leads to inferences of
  manipulative intent and source derogation ... We confirm that, on average, clickbait articles elicit
  far fewer shares than non-clickbait articles. Our results are stable, with large effect sizes".
- Molyneux & Coddington, *Journalism Practice* 14(4), https://doi.org/10.1080/17512786.2019.1628658 :
  "the effect of clickbait headlines on story credibility and quality is negative but rather small --
  small enough that this study did not have enough statistical power to detect it at a level of
  significance."
- Facebook 2016 (https://about.fb.com/news/2016/08/news-feed-fyi-further-reducing-clickbait-in-feed/):
  clickbait = "(1) if the headline withholds information required to understand what the content of the
  article is; and (2) if the headline exaggerates the article to create misleading expectations."
  Example it penalises: "You'll Never Believe Who Tripped and Fell on the Red Carpet...".
  Facebook 2014 measured it by the ratio of clicks to likes/comments and by whether people "come
  straight back to News Feed".
- TikTok: its For You feed standards are reported (secondary sources) to make "tricks to boost
  engagement (e.g., 'like-for-like' promises)" ineligible for recommendation. I could not load the
  primary page (JavaScript-only), so treat the exact TikTok wording as **unverified**.

**The "pays off" vs "deceptive" line, in evidence terms:**
- A gap that is *resolved* inside the content is net-positive for the viewer (Ruan et al., 7 studies) -
  this is the curiosity gap that pays off.
- A headline that *withholds the subject* or *exaggerates* is (a) what platforms demote, (b) read as
  manipulative and shared less (Mukherjee), (c) clicked but not liked (Qiu & Golman; Facebook's own
  click-to-like signal). For a carousel, where the algorithm reads swipes, dwell, likes, saves and
  shares - not the tap - the payoff part is not optional.

## 7. Thumbnail text, image text, and the 1-2 second glance

| Finding | Source | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Strong sentiment in thumbnails -> more views; caps, punctuation, emoticons -> more clicks | Cui et al. 2024, 16,215 YouTube covers | B (observational) |
| More information in YouTube titles -> fewer views; negative emotional intensity in titles -> more views | Tafesse 2020, 4,398 videos | B- |
| Thumbnail aesthetics/informativeness vs view-through, 3,745 brand videos (no text-specific result in abstract) | Koh & Cui 2022, *Decision Support Systems* https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dss.2022.113820 | B |
| Photos with faces: "38% more likely to receive likes and 32% more likely to receive comments" (1M Instagram images) | Bakhshi, Shamma & Gilbert 2014, CHI https://doi.org/10.1145/2556288.2557403 | B |
| TikTok news videos (101,292): negative sentiment and second-person *camera view* shots -> more likes/comments/shares, weaker for news publishers | Cheng & Li 2024, *Social Science Computer Review* https://doi.org/10.1177/08944393231178603 | B |
| Feed dwell: "1.7 seconds with a piece of content on mobile compared to 2.5 seconds on desktop"; recall after 0.25 s | Facebook IQ, "Capturing Attention in Feed" (2016), via https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/facebook-releases-new-guide-to-help-advertisers-maximize-video-ad-performan/450818 | C (platform internal) |
| Scene meaning extracted at 13 ms per picture | Potter et al. 2014, *Attention, Perception & Psychophysics* https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-013-0605-z | A (lab, replicated RSVP paradigm) |

| Thumbnails with 3-5 words of text beat text-free in search, lose in browse; "0-3 words" outperform | thumbnail-tool blogs (thumbai.dev, tubeanalytics.net) | D |

Verbatim anchors:
- Potter et al.: "performance was significantly above chance at all durations" (13-80 ms per picture).
- Brysbaert: "we estimate that the average silent reading rate for adults in English is 238 words per
  minute (wpm) for non-fiction ... Reading rates in other languages can be predicted reasonably well by
  taking into account the number of words these languages require to convey the same message".

**Arithmetic (derived, C):** 238 wpm = ~4 words/second. In a 1.7 s mobile dwell, a viewer who spends
the first ~0.3 s on the photo (the image is parsed in tens of ms) has time for **~5-6 short words**.
A 10-word line will usually be only partly read. Hebrew packs prepositions and articles into words, so
the same message takes fewer Hebrew words but those words are longer - count characters, not just words.

## 9. Rules that survive - writing a <=10-word cover line for a TikTok photo carousel

Ranked by (strength of evidence) x (how directly it transfers to a carousel cover). Each rule names the
grade of the evidence *behind it*; the transfer to TikTok itself is always at most one grade lower,
because no study tested this exact surface.

1. **Name a concrete thing; withhold exactly one specific detail about it.** One gap, one missing
   piece, closable on the next slides. Not "this will change your trip" (vague, Facebook-demoted), not a
   full summary (no gap). *Evidence: A- (Loewenstein mechanism; Kang inverted-U; Golman "specifically
   what they don't know"; Menon & Soman "hint"; Banerjee concreteness).*

2. **Make the viewer feel they *almost* know the answer.** Pick a gap where the audience can half-guess
   (~50/50): a place they've heard of, a habit they have, a choice they've made. Inviting a guess
   ("Guess which...", a ranked pair to compare) adds pull. *Evidence: A-/C (Kang 2009 peak at P = .5;
   Wade & Kidd 2019 perceived closeness r = .23; Brod & Breitwieser 2019 prediction raises curiosity).*

3. **Keep it readable in one glance: aim for 4-7 short words, the key noun in the first two.** The
   10-word ceiling is a maximum, not a target. *Evidence: C (derived: 1.7 s mobile dwell, 238 wpm,
   NN/g "about 2 words"; Kuiken and Jung: short words win). The Upworthy "longer wins" result does not
   transfer to a 1.7 s glance.*

4. **Pay the gap off inside the carousel - early, and fully.** The algorithm sees swipes, dwell, saves
   and shares, not just the stop. Unresolved or oversold covers get the tap and lose the like and the
   share. *Evidence: B+/A- (Mukherjee 2022 fewer shares + manipulation inference; Qiu & Golman
   clicks vs likes; Ruan 2018 tease-then-resolve is net-positive; Facebook click-to-like detection).*

5. **Swap upbeat adjectives for intensity.** Cut "amazing / beautiful / best / dream". Use a concrete,
   emotionally charged fact or contrast instead. *Evidence: A- (positive words -1.0% per word on
   Upworthy; positive tone negative across 398 Chartbeat sites; emotional intensity positive in both;
   superlatives no better than plain in Molina).*

6. **Mild negative framing ("the mistake", "wrong", "nobody tells you") only when it is true and
   the audience is browsing for fun; never fear or anger.** On an entertainment feed this is likely
   neutral-to-positive; with readers who want reliable info it reads as manipulation. Sadness
   helped, fear hurt, anger did nothing on Upworthy. *Evidence: B (Robertson A within Upworthy;
   Banerjee & Urminsky 2025 null average + backfire for credibility-seekers).*

7. **Write a statement that implies the question; do not end with "?".** *Evidence: C against questions
   (Kuiken -17 pp; Molina OR 1.97 for plain over question; Scacco & Muddiman more negative reaction;
   Banerjee "answer a question" cues worse). Only Lai & Farbrot (small) found the opposite.*

8. **Prefer a specific other person or place over "you".** "This couple", "a local", "she" are better
   supported than "you". Use "you" only where it sounds natural. *Evidence: A null for "you" (Gligoric
   H7); A for third-person singular (beta 0.22); C for "social stories about individuals" (Banerjee prelim).*

9. **No stock bait phrases.** "You won't believe", "what happened next", "this will blow your mind" are
   classifier fingerprints, are what platforms demote, and were associated with ~a quarter fewer
   reactions/shares. *Evidence: B- (Jung 2022; Facebook 2016 definition; Chakraborty: 65% of clickbait
   reuses the same top n-grams).*

10. **Put the emphasis on the image, not in the caption.** Caps on one word, a single emoji, a strong
    photo with a face or a striking scene. Keep the caption calmer. *Evidence: B/B- (Cui 2024
    thumbnails vs captions; Bakhshi faces +38% likes; Jung: unusual punctuation helps in the headline,
    hurts in the body).*

11. **A concrete number is fine; do not choose it for being odd.** *Evidence: C for numbers (mixed);
    D - folklore - for odd-vs-even.*

12. **No puns.** *Evidence: C (Lockwood 2016: wordplay titles drew less attention).*

13. **Treat every rule above as a prior, then A/B the cover.** Linguistic features predict the winning
    headline only 54% of the time; topic and photo dominate. Post two cover variants of the same deck
    over time and keep the one with higher swipe-through, not higher views. *Evidence: A (Gligoric
    54.42%; Kuiken: no model beat baseline; Banerjee: literature direction right only about half the time).*

**Hebrew shape examples** (illustrate rules 1-3, 5, 7, 8; they make no price or product claim):

- "המפרץ שהמקומיים מסתירים מהתיירים" (concrete place, one missing detail, third party, no question mark)
- "הטעות שכולם עושים ביום הראשון ברומא" (mild negative, specific, almost-knowable)
- "נחשו איזה מהשניים הוא באמת באיטליה" (invites a prediction - rule 2)

Avoid: "המקום הכי מדהים בעולם" (positive superlative, no gap), "אתם לא תאמינו מה יש שם" (stock bait,
withholds the subject), "האם זה החוף הכי יפה באירופה?" (question + superlative).

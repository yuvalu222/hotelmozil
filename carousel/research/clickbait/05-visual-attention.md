# 05 — The cover as a picture: how image and text compete for a glance

Researched 10.10. Scope: the perception side of the first slide — how fast it is
judged, how much text a glance can carry, what the eye lands on, how the text
must be drawn to survive the photo and the small surfaces, and what "native"
type on an iPhone actually is. Wording is out of scope (see `02-hook.md`,
`scroll-stop/LITERATURE.md`).

**Grades.** A = controlled experiment (random assignment or lab manipulation).
B = large observational dataset, or a platform's own internal test reported
without full method. C = small observational / qualitative. D = opinion,
vendor blog, tutorial, or a number with no method. A platform reporting on its
own product is marked "(owner-reported)" — it is the best source on how the
product works and the weakest on whether it works.

Every quote below was read on the page cited in this session unless marked
otherwise. Where a page could not be fetched, that is said.

---

## 1. How fast a cover is judged

| # | Claim | Source | Verbatim | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | Meaning can be extracted from a picture shown for 13 ms | Potter, Wyble, Hagmann, McCourt (2014), *Attention, Perception & Psychophysics* 76(2) — pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24374558 | "performance was significantly above chance at all durations, whether the target was named before or only after the sequence" (durations 13-80 ms) | A |
| 1.2 | A visual liking judgment forms in 50 ms and colours later judgments | Lindgaard, Fernandes, Dudek, Brown (2006), *Behaviour & IT* 25:115-126 (summary via websiteoptimization.com/speed/tweak/blink) | web pages flashed "for 500 msec and 50 msec"; judgments at 50 ms; halo onto usability/credibility | A (web pages, not photos) |
| 1.3 | Mobile feed content is recalled after a quarter second | Facebook IQ, "Capturing Attention in Feed" (2016) — facebook.com/business/news/insights/capturing-attention-feed-video-creative | "it takes only 0.25 seconds of exposure for people to recall mobile feed content at a statistically significant rate" | B (owner-reported; Fors Marsh Group did the recall work per marketingdive.com) |
| 1.4 | Average time per post in the mobile feed: 1.7 s | same page | "1.7 seconds with a piece of content on mobile compared to 2.5 seconds on desktop" | B (owner-reported, 2016, Facebook feed, not TikTok) |
| 1.5 | Netflix: 1.8 s per title; artwork is most of what is looked at | Netflix, "The Power of a Picture" (2016) — about.netflix.com/en/news/the-power-of-a-picture | "users spent an average of 1.8 seconds considering each title"; artwork "constituted over 82% of their focus while browsing Netflix" | B (owner-reported consumer research) |

**What this means for the cover.** The photo is processed before any word is
read: gist at tens of ms, a like/dislike in ~50 ms, recall at 250 ms. The text
gets whatever is left of a ~1.7-1.8 s look — and only if the picture earned the
look. The photo decides *whether* the text is read; the text decides what the
viewer does next.

## 2. How many words a glance can carry

| # | Claim | Source | Verbatim | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | Adult silent reading ≈ 238 wpm (English non-fiction) | Brysbaert (2019), *J. Memory and Language* 109:104047 | "Based on the analysis of 190 studies (18,573 participants), we estimate that the average silent reading rate for adults in English is 238 words per minute (wpm) for non-fiction and 260 wpm for fiction." | A (meta-analysis) |
| 2.2 | Hebrew silent reading ≈ 224 wpm; Hebrew needs ~782 words for 1,000 English | same paper, table of languages | row "Hebrew 5 168 224 2 83 147 782" (silent: 5 studies, 168 participants, 224 wpm; expansion index 782) | A (meta-analytic table; Hebrew n is small) |
| 2.3 | A fixation lasts ~200-250 ms | Rayner (1998), *Psychological Bulletin* — via search summary, not fetched | "fixations typically last about 200-250 ms" | A (review; secondary quote) |
| 2.4 | Web users read ~20% of the words on a page | Nielsen Norman Group, "How Little Do Users Read" — nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read | users "read about 20% of the text on the average page" (search-result quote; page not fetched) | B (50,000 page views) |
| 2.5 | Too much text over a Pin cut performance 42% | Pinterest fashion study via WWD (paywalled; not fetched) | "Having too much text over an image resulted in 42 percent lower performance" (search summary; 10,000+ Promoted Pins, 1,000 advertisers) | B, owner-reported; "too much" undefined |
| 2.6 | YouTube: keep thumbnails simple, text easy to read | support.google.com/youtube/answer/12340300 | "Try not to make the design too complex. Dynamic use of color and composition can help catch the eye, but too much can overwhelm it." · "If you add text, make sure to use a font that's easy to read." | D (owner advice, no data) |
| 2.7 | Our own Hebrew corpus: long covers lose | `scroll-stop/COVERS-HE.md` (1,117 Hebrew carousels) | "Long cover text (≥15 words): ×0.60 [0.40, 0.90]" | B (own data, cross-section) |

**Arithmetic (mine, not a source).** 1.7 s × 224 wpm ÷ 60 = **6.3 Hebrew words**
— and that is the ceiling, because it assumes the whole look goes to reading
and none to the photo. Netflix's 1.8 s gives 6.7. Six to seven Hebrew words is
the most a cover can ask of a viewer who has not yet decided to stop; the
words beyond that are read only by someone who already stopped.
Falsify by: a within-creator comparison of cover word count against plays in
`harvest/` — if 10-14 word covers do as well as 4-7, the ceiling is wrong.

## 3. What the eye lands on inside the frame

### 3a. Faces and text are the two magnets

| # | Claim | Source | Verbatim | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3.1 | Faces and text pull gaze far beyond their size, even when told not to look | Cerf, Frady, Koch (2009), *Journal of Vision* 9(12) — authors.library.caltech.edu/40670 | "subjects in free-viewing conditions look at faces and text 16.6 and 11.1 times more than similar regions normalized for size and position" · "In terms of attracting gaze, text is almost as effective as faces." · "it is difficult to avoid looking at faces and text even when doing so imposes a cost" | A (small lab n) |
| 3.2 | Text in scenes attracts attention by its visual shape, more so in a familiar script | Wang & Pomplun (2012), *Journal of Vision* — pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3845417 | "specific visual features of texts, rather than typically salient features (e.g., color, orientation, and contrast), are the main attractors of attention; (d) the meaningfulness of texts does not add to their attentional capture; and (e) the attraction of attention depends to some extent on the observer's familiarity with the writing system" | A (small lab n) |
| 3.3 | A face looking at the camera holds the eye on the face; a face looking away sends it to the text | Sajjacholapunt & Ball (2014), *Frontiers in Psychology* 5:166 — pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3941030 | "faces with averted gaze increased attention to the banner overall, as well as to the advertising text and product" · "in the condition involving faces with mutual gaze, the focus of attention was localized more on the face region rather than on the text or product, weakening any memory benefits" | A (n = 72; banner ads on desktop) |

Implication: on a cover that has both a face and a hook line, they compete. A
face staring into the lens wins the glance and the hook may go unread; a person
looking into the scene (or seen from behind) leaves the eye free for the words.
Hebrew text is a strong magnet for Hebrew readers specifically (3.2e).

### 3b. Does a person in the photo help? Platform says yes, travel says careful

| # | Claim | Source | Verbatim | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3.4 | Instagram photos with faces got more likes and comments | Bakhshi, Shamma, Gilbert (CHI 2014) — news.gatech.edu/news/2014/03/20/face-it-instagram-pictures-faces-are-more-popular | "1.1 million photos on Instagram" · "38 percent more likely to receive likes than photos with no faces" · "32 percent more likely to attract comments" · "the number of faces in the photo, their age or gender didn't make a difference" | B |
| 3.5 | Brand posts: faces helped on Twitter, did nothing on Instagram; professional quality helped everywhere | Li & Xie (2020), *Journal of Marketing Research* — via kellercenter.hankamer.baylor.edu | "Pictures with human faces can increase engagement measures by 38% to 291% on Twitter, such an effect is absent on Instagram." · "High-quality and professionally shot images consistently lead to higher engagement regardless of platform or product category." · "Colorful pictures increase engagement for air-travel-related posts" | B (airline + SUV brand posts) |
| 3.6 | Travel/venue photos: another person in the shot can LOWER liking of the place | Lu, Jung, Peck (2024), *Journal of Consumer Research* 51(2):321-341 — ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v51y2024i2p321-341..html | "the presence (vs. absence) of another human in the photo of an identity-relevant experience...can lower viewers' liking and preference for the venue" — "mediated by viewers' feelings of others' ownership of the venue"; moderated by identity relevance and "the distinctiveness of the human in the photo" | A + B (5 experiments + 14,725 Instagram photos of a top travel influencer) |
| 3.7 | Human presence lowered attractiveness of cultural places, not natural ones | Cui et al. (2026), *Behavioral Sciences* — via synapsesocial.com summary | human presence "lowers place attractiveness by diminishing feelings of groundedness—an effect observed in cultural landscapes but not natural ones" | A/C (abstract only; small journal) |
| 3.8 | Real people are scrutinised; generic stock people are ignored | NN/g, "Photos as Web Content" — nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content | "Some types of pictures are completely ignored. This is typically the case for big feel-good images that are purely decorative." · users "ignore stock photos of generic people" · "Photos of products and real people (as opposed to stock photos of models)" are scrutinised | C (qualitative eye-tracking) |

Reconciling 3.4-3.7: a face raises *attention to the post* (3.1, 3.4) but, in a
destination photo, a distinct stranger can lower *wanting to be there* (3.6,
3.7). For a hotel/destination cover the goal is the second. The safe forms are
the ones that keep the place "available": no person, a small or rear-view
figure that the viewer can project into, or a nature scene (where 3.7 found no
penalty). This matches the owner's standing cover rule (sunset/sea/person) only
if "person" means a non-distinct figure, not a face filling the frame.
Falsify by: our own harvest — code cover photos as {no person, rear/small
figure, frontal face} and compare within creator.

## 4. Making the text survive the photo

| # | Claim | Source | Verbatim | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4.1 | Poor contrast makes readers skip | NN/g, "Ensure High Contrast for Text Over Images" — nngroup.com/articles/text-over-images | "When text is difficult to decipher, readers are forced to choose between straining their eyes and skipping over the content." · design must "provide a high enough contrast for the worst-case background image and text placement" | C/D (guideline) |
| 4.2 | Contrast threshold | same (citing WCAG) | "at least 4.5:1 (or 3:1 for large characters...)" | standard |
| 4.3 | In a glance, dark-on-light reads faster than light-on-dark; humanist faces faster; small type hurts more for some faces | Dobres, Chahine, Reimer (2016/17), *Ergonomics* — pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5213401 | "black-on-white text is significantly more legible than white-on-black" (thresholds 38.6% lower) · humanist 8.8% shorter exposure needed · 4 mm to 3 mm cap height raised thresholds "26.4%" (humanist) vs "62.1%" (square grotesque) · ~"70ms for 20-year-olds versus 126ms for 65-year-olds" | A |
| 4.4 | Fluent reading needs at least ~0.2° of x-height | Legge & Bigelow (2011), *Journal of Vision* — pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3428264 | "The fluent range extends over a factor of 10 in angular print size (x-height) from approximately 0.2° to 2°. Assuming a standard reading distance of 40 cm (16 inches), the corresponding physical x-heights are 1.4 mm (4 points) and 14 mm (40 points)." | A (review of experiments) |
| 4.5 | Apple's smallest recommended iOS text | Apple HIG Typography (developer.apple.com JSON of the page) | "Default size: 17 pt" · "Minimum size: 11 pt" | standard |
| 4.6 | Box > outline > shadow over busy video | blitzcutai.com (vendor), citing W3C/BBC/Netflix | "Background box is the most readable caption treatment, text outline is second, and drop shadow is third." | D (no study found in this session) |

Implication for white-with-black-outline (the owner's style): white text alone
is the weak polarity (4.3), and on a bright sky or white sand it has no
contrast at all. The black outline is what supplies the contrast — it turns
every glyph edge into dark-on-light locally. So the outline is not decoration;
its thickness is the legibility setting. The box (4.6) would be safer on chaotic
backgrounds; the owner chose the outline, so the remedy is placement: put the
line over the calmest, most uniform region of the photo (sky, water, wall), not
over foliage or crowds (4.1 "worst-case background").

## 5. TikTok surfaces: where the cover is seen and how big the words are

| # | Claim | Source | Verbatim | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1 | Photo Mode: images auto-advance, viewer can swipe; 2,200-char captions | TikTok newsroom 6.10.2022 via socialmediatoday.com | "carousel posts of still images that automatically display one after another ... which viewers can swipe through at their own pace" | owner-reported |
| 5.2 | Safe zone is not one size | TikTok Ads help — ads.tiktok.com/help/article/tiktok-auction-in-feed-ads | "The safe zone size is determined by the dimension (vertical, horizontal, or square), ad caption length, and any additional formats used." | owner-reported (ads) |
| 5.3 | Typical In-Feed overlay areas on 1080x1920 | adkit.so / trymypost.com (third-party, from TikTok overlay files) | top ~254 px (13.2%), bottom ~707 px (36.8%), right action column ~242 px (22.4%), left ~122 px | D |
| 5.4 | Profile grid crops covers toward 3:4, 3 columns | moda.app, nemovideo.com | "thumbnails are cropped to a roughly 3:4 vertical rectangle" in a "3-column layout" | D |
| 5.5 | iPhone 16 screen | apple.com/iphone-16/specs | "2556-by-1179-pixel resolution at 460 ppi" (= 393 pt wide at 3x) | spec |

**Computed sizes (mine).** His measured cover text: Hebrew body height ≈ 55 px
on a 1440 px-wide 3:4 frame (3.8% of width), line ≈ 75% of width
(`harvest/own-full/7680815617800162580`, `.../7680583402306833684`). On an
iPhone 16 (393 pt; 1 pt ≈ 0.166 mm):

| surface | frame width on screen | body height | physical | angle at 30 / 40 cm | vs 0.2° fluent floor / 11 pt |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| For You, full screen | ~393 pt | ~15 pt | ~2.5 mm | 0.48° / 0.36° | readable |
| 2-column grid (search; column count not verified for photo results) | ~196 pt | ~7.5 pt | ~1.2 mm | 0.24° / 0.18° | at the edge, below 11 pt |
| 3-column profile grid | ~131 pt | ~5 pt | ~0.8 mm | 0.16° / 0.12° | below the floor |

So in the grids the cover is effectively a picture with a grey smear where the
text is — the photo carries the tap there, exactly the Netflix "too complex at
small sizes" finding (3.11). To be fluent-readable in the profile grid the body
would need ≈ 70 px on 1440 (≈ 4.8% of width); to reach Apple's 11 pt in a
2-column grid ≈ 80 px; in the 3-column grid ≈ 120 px — which leaves room for
only 2-4 Hebrew words per line. Choice: either the cover hook is short and big
enough for the grid, or accept that the grid sells the photo alone.
Also from 5.3: keep the text out of the bottom ~37% and the right ~22% of the
feed frame (caption, username, buttons); the owner's top-third placement
already does this. Hebrew is right-aligned by reading direction but the right
column is TikTok's button rail — a centred line is the safe default.

## 6. Native-looking beats produced? Mostly, with a caveat

| # | Claim | Source | Verbatim | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6.1 | TikTok-native ads lifted intent; inauthentic "native" ads lost 19% | MAGNA x TikTok (2021) — magnaglobal.com/entertaining-and-authentic-tiktok-ads-perform-best-with-consumers | "4,447 regular TikTok users in a mobile online panel across two markets (US, UK)" randomly assigned in a "controlled TikTok environment" · native = videos that "look and feel like TikTok content" · inauthentic native ads "19% less effective in driving purchase intent" · new customers watched native ads "27% longer than average" | A (randomised panel; TikTok-commissioned; ads, video) |
| 6.2 | Same study: polished repurposed ads were more memorable | same | "Repurposed ads showed +37% memorability gains when high-quality" (fetch summary) | A, same caveats |
| 6.3 | Meta/Toluna Reels: native elements (emoji, direct-to-camera, lo-fi) beat heavy production | ppc.land and tolunacorporate.com reporting Meta's 2.12.2025 release | "roughly 100 ads, 100 viewers per ad"; "top 20% of performing ads against the average"; "Every one of these findings is a probability driver, not a rule" | B/C (100 ads; not randomised by element) |
| 6.4 | Emoji as a native element | Meta for Business — facebook.com/business/news/reels-creative-strategies | "Using emojis as a native element makes creatives 2.5x more likely to rank in the top 20% for purchase intent." | B/C |
| 6.5 | Counter-evidence: professional photo quality raised engagement | Li & Xie (2020), 3.5 above | "High-quality and professionally shot images consistently lead to higher engagement" | B |

Reading: "native" means *made in the platform's own idiom* (its fonts, its text
tool, its emoji, phone-shot framing), not "low quality". 6.1 and 6.5 do not
conflict once separated: a sharp, well-exposed phone photo with the platform's
own text treatment is native and high-quality at once. What loses is the
designed look — brand fonts, gradients, boxes and layouts nobody can make in
the app (and 6.1's "inauthentic native" — a fake-native imitation — is the
worst cell). This is the strongest argument for drawing text exactly the way
TikTok's own editor draws it.

**What follows.** When Hebrew is typed in TikTok's text tool on an iPhone, the
Hebrew letters cannot come from TikTok Sans (7.3); iOS substitutes its system
Hebrew. Latin letters, digits and punctuation in the same line ("10", "?", "!")
stay in TikTok Sans. **Which Hebrew face iOS substitutes is not documented by
Apple anywhere I could find.** Apple names SF Hebrew as a member of the system
family (7.5), which makes SF Hebrew the likely answer on current iOS; Arial
Hebrew is the older candidate. The iOS version at which the system Hebrew
became SF Hebrew is not established.

**Measured on his own slides (this session).** Two of his covers were compared
glyph-by-glyph against SF Hebrew and Arial (Windows), using statistics that the
outline cannot distort (glyph-centre positions):

| font | rms of glyph centres, slide A / slide B |
|---|---|
| SF Hebrew Regular | 0.0024 / 0.0025 |
| SF Hebrew Light | 0.0024 / 0.0030 |
| Arial (Windows) | 0.0025 / 0.0029 |
| SF Hebrew Medium | 0.0037 / 0.0035 |
| SF Hebrew Semibold | 0.0053 / 0.0032 |
| SF Hebrew Bold | 0.0067 / 0.0037 |

Result: **regular weight, not bold** (bold weights fit clearly worse on slide
A). **SF Hebrew vs Arial-style cannot be separated at the resolution of his
slides** — the two designs are near-identical in proportion. Word spaces in his
text measure 0.46-0.53 of the letter height, which matches SF Hebrew letters
with TikTok Sans's wider space (0.47-0.53) — consistent with the mixed-font run
described above, but also matched by Arial's own space (0.49-0.57). Confidence
that it is SF Hebrew: moderate (Apple's own naming plus a fit no worse than
Arial). How to settle it: an on-device CoreText dump on any current iPhone
(`CTFontCopyDefaultCascadeListForLanguages` for "he", or the font name CoreText
assigns to a Hebrew run), or a lossless full-resolution export of a line typed
in the text tool at a large size, compared glyph by glyph — the JPEG slides in
`harvest/` are too soft for it.

Practical consequence, whichever it is: SF Hebrew's licence forbids using it to
render posts (7.9). `native/fonts/SF-Hebrew.ttf` is in the repo; `native/render.mjs`
does not load it (checked) and it should stay that way. The renderer's
measured choice (Open Sans 600 for Hebrew, TikTok Sans for Latin/digits, from
`harvest/own-full/fontcand`) is the right structure. My centre-spacing test
suggests checking Open Sans 400-500 against 600, since his glyphs fit
regular-weight Hebrew best.

### 7b. The outline ("stroke")

| # | Claim | Source | Verbatim | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7.10 | TikTok's text tool has a stroke style and a background style, cycled by an icon | minter.io; tutkit.com | "A stroke effect adds an outline to the text in a contrasting colour" · "A background effect adds a coloured rectangle behind the text." · "Cycle through options by pressing the text effect icon on the left-hand side of the screen." | D (tutorials; TikTok's own help page for the text tool was not retrievable) |

Whether TikTok draws the stroke centred over the fill (eating into it) or
behind it cannot be decided from a photo of the result; the regular-weight fit

fill with a white outline (`render.mjs` comment, measured earlier).

Emoji: the iPhone's own Apple Color Emoji (the renderer already uses it); Meta's
2.5x (6.4) is the only quantified support for emoji as a native signal.

## 8. Conflicts and gaps — not settled by this research

* **Faces.** Instagram-wide data says faces lift likes (3.4); brand data says
  not on Instagram (3.5); travel experiments say a distinct person lowers
  wanting the place (3.6). For destination covers, 3.6 is the closest match.
* **Punctuation/caps.** Observational YouTube (3.13) vs randomised Upworthy:
  the randomised result wins.
* **Grid column count on TikTok search for photo posts** is not verified from a
  primary source; the size table assumes 2 columns.
* **TikTok feed dwell time** has no public number; the 1.7 s is Facebook 2016.
* **iOS Hebrew system font history** — Apple does not document which version
  switched to SF Hebrew; on-device test proposed in 7a.
* No randomised study was found on outline vs box vs shadow over photos; 4.6 is
  vendor opinion.

## 9. Rules for the cover — Hebrew travel photo carousel

1. **The photo is the stopper; the text is the reason to stay.** Judge the
   photo alone at thumbnail size first (it is judged in < 0.25 s, 1.1-1.3, and
   it is all the grid shows, §5).
2. **One clear subject, at most three people, nothing that needs to be large
   to make sense** (3.11). Wide vistas with a single focal point — sea, sunset,
   a pool edge — survive the grid; busy streets do not.
3. **No distinct stranger owning the place.** If a person appears, make them
   small, rear-view or looking into the scene — never a frontal face filling
   the frame (3.3, 3.6). Nature scenes tolerate people better than city scenes
   (3.7).
4. **Six Hebrew words is the ceiling for the hook** that must be read before
   the stop (§2: 1.7 s × 224 wpm); ≥15 words measured ×0.60 in our corpus. More
   words may follow only in a smaller second line meant for those who stopped.
5. **Size for the surface you want to win.** Feed-only: body ≥ ~55 px on 1440
   (current). If the cover must also read in search or the profile grid: the
   key 2-4 words at ≥ ~80 px body (≈ 5.5% of width), §5.
6. **Place the text over the calmest region of the photo** — sky, water, a wall
   — in the top third, centred, clear of the bottom ~37% and the right ~22%
   (5.3). The outline supplies contrast; it cannot rescue text over foliage or
   crowds (4.1, 4.3).
7. **Draw it the way TikTok's editor does — nothing the app cannot make**
   (6.1): white fill, pure black outline ≈ 0.08 em visible with round joins,
   regular-weight Hebrew in a system-like sans (SF Hebrew look; licensed
   stand-in), TikTok Sans for digits and Latin, Apple emoji. No gradients,
   boxes, brand fonts, or a second colour beyond the single accent word.
8. **One emotion, legible in the photo or the emoji** (3.9, 3.12, 3.13, 6.4) —
   but no "!", no ALL-CAPS (randomised evidence, `scroll-stop/LITERATURE.md`).
9. **Text and photo must say the same thing** — name the place the photo shows.
   The words are read only after the picture has made a promise; a mismatch
   breaks it. (Supported indirectly: destination-naming covers ×2.57 in
   `COVERS-HE.md`; image-text fit helped on Twitter in 3.5, not Instagram — so
   treat as moderate.)
10. **Test it as a thumbnail before posting:** shrink the cover to 131 px wide
    (profile grid) and 196 px (search). If the photo does not still say
    "beautiful place" at that size, change the photo, not the text.
